**Assistant:**

A fresher can't be taken to 20-year expert level in a fixed timeline, and no plan can guarantee it. Expertise in macro trading comes from living through full market cycles, making real decisions with real money, and being wrong often enough to learn from it. A training plan can shorten the path and make the learning deliberate, but the last stretch is time-bound. What follows is the plan I'd build: a structured progression with explicit milestones, so that the trainee's progress can be measured rather than assumed.

### Guiding principles

1. **Market time is non-negotiable.** Cycles take years. The plan should get a trainee into live markets early, with small risk, and keep them there.
2. **Every concept needs a trade or a test.** Reading about funding markets isn't enough. The trainee should explain the concept, forecast something with it, and check the result.
3. **Judgement is trained through review.** Keep a decision journal, review it weekly and quarterly, and separate good process from good outcomes.
4. **Risk management comes before returns.** A trainee who blows up early never reaches the later stages. Every stage has a loss limit.
5. **Build breadth first, then depth.** Start with one market, then add others. Experts tend to be deep in a few areas and conversant in many.

### Step-by-step plan

#### Phase 0: Foundations (months 0–3)

**Goal:** Fluent in the mechanics of rates, FX, and basic derivatives.

- Build pricing skills: bond maths, duration and DV01, forward rates, curve bootstrapping, swap valuation.
- Learn the quoting conventions for each instrument and how to read a Bloomberg-style screen.
- Build a Python and Excel toolkit for pulling data, calculating DV01, and running simple scenarios.
- Keep a daily market journal: three market moves, the cause, and what surprised you.

**Milestone:** The trainee can price a vanilla swap, a bond, and an FX forward by hand, and build a DV01-neutral curve trade in a spreadsheet.

#### Phase 1: Macro and policy reasoning (months 3–9)

**Goal:** Forecast data and central bank responses, and be right for the right reasons.

- Study the reaction functions of the Fed, ECB, BoE, BoJ, and one emerging market central bank. Read minutes, speeches, and dot plots.
- Build a forecast of inflation and growth for each economy, and score the forecasts against the data each month.
- Study how data releases move markets, and how much of the move was already priced.

**Milestone:** Three months of forecasts scored against outcomes, with a written review of misses.

#### Phase 2: Funding, plumbing, and liquidity (months 9–15)

**Goal:** Understand the mechanics that drive the moves experts respond to first.

- Learn repo, SOFR, tri-party versus bilateral funding, and the Treasury basis trade.
- Study the cross-currency basis, dollar funding stress, and dealer balance sheet constraints such as the leverage ratio.
- Replay stress episodes (2008, March 2020, the 2022 gilt crisis, the 2024 repo pressure) and identify where funding broke first.

**Milestone:** The trainee can explain, with a diagram and a numerical example, how a funding squeeze would move the basis, swap spreads, and the Treasury curve.

#### Phase 3: Volatility and convexity (months 15–22)

**Goal:** Price and hedge optionality, and understand how dealer hedging moves markets.

- Learn Black and Bachelier models, the volatility surface, skew, vanna and volga.
- Build and calibrate a simple SABR model across two regimes, and compare the fits.
- Study convexity in mortgage markets and the hedging flows it generates.

**Milestone:** The trainee builds an options book that is delta- and vega-hedged, and explains the P&L attribution daily.

#### Phase 4: Risk and portfolio construction (months 22–30)

**Goal:** Size positions, set limits, and survive drawdowns.

- Build a risk system: DV01, VaR, expected shortfall, scenario analysis, and liquidity-adjusted exit horizons.
- Practise drawdown management: define loss limits, cut rules, and rebuild rules.
- Study correlation regime shifts and how to stress them.

**Milestone:** The trainee runs a live or simulated book with a written risk budget, and a documented response to a planned stress test.

#### Phase 5: Live trading with supervision (months 30–48)

**Goal:** Make real decisions with real risk, under supervision.

- Start with a small allocation and a strict loss limit. Increase size only after a set number of months of positive risk-adjusted results and process review.
- Require a written thesis, entry, stop, target, and invalidation for every trade, reviewed by a senior trader.
- Rotate across instruments: rates, FX, commodities, and EM, as the trainee demonstrates competence in each.

**Milestone:** Two years of supervised trading with a documented track record, reviewed for both process and outcome.

#### Phase 6: Independent judgement and cycle experience (years 4–10)

**Goal:** Build the pattern recognition that only comes from living through regimes.

- Hold independent risk with a defined budget and a senior reviewer.
- Trade through at least one full regime change and one major stress event, and write a post-mortem on each.
- Build a mentor network across desks and regions, and learn from traders who have lived through different cycles.

**Milestone:** A decade of track record through at least two regimes, with post-mortems on each.

#### Phase 7: Expertise (years 10–20+)

**Goal:** Develop the judgement that separates an experienced trader from an expert.

- Manage large capital and teams, and make decisions where the data is ambiguous.
- Study the history of every major crisis and policy regime, and maintain a personal library of analogues.
- Mentor others, which forces you to articulate and test your own judgement.

**Milestone:** Recognised as a senior decision-maker across regimes, with a verifiable track record.

### What needs to be covered

### How to measure progress

- **Quantitative:** risk-adjusted return, drawdown, forecast accuracy, and calibration error on models.
- **Process:** share of trades with a complete thesis and invalidation level, and how often stops were followed.
- **Judgement:** the quality of post-mortems, and whether the trainee's views change correctly when the data changes.
- **Cycle experience:** the number of distinct regimes traded and reviewed.

Use these measures at each milestone. If the trainee isn't meeting a milestone, extend that phase rather than moving on.

### Honest limits

- **Time cannot be compressed.** Two years of supervised trading is a minimum for most desks, and cycle experience takes more.
- **Some judgement is learned only by losing.** A plan can reduce the cost of losses by keeping risk small, but it can't replace them.
- **Mentorship matters more than curriculum.** The best accelerator is working next to people who have already made the mistakes.

For a macro trader with more than five years in the seat, the day-to-day view is less about single data points and more about a running set of questions. The questions below are the ones I'd expect a senior macro trader to check every morning, and the views they'd want to see on a screen or in a note.

### 1. The regime view: where are we in the cycle?

This is the first thing a senior trader looks at, because it sets how every other signal should be read.

- Growth direction: accelerating, stable, or slowing, using a few leading indicators rather than one headline number.
- Inflation direction: is it heading toward target, stuck above it, or re-accelerating?
- Whether the current regime is confirming or breaking what the market is pricing.
- A simple regime label, such as "reflation," "disinflationary slowdown," or "stagflation," with the evidence behind it.

The point is to know whether today's data is a continuation of the regime or the start of a new one.

### 2. The policy path: what is priced versus what I expect

- The market-implied policy path from fed funds futures, SOFR futures, and OIS, for the next four to six meetings.
- The house view on the same path, with the difference shown in basis points.
- The central bank communication that matters most this week: speeches, minutes, and any scheduled blackout periods.
- The reaction function: what would make the central bank move faster or slower than expected.

A trade idea is only interesting when the gap between priced and expected is large enough to be worth the risk.

### 3. The data calendar and surprise sensitivity

- The release schedule for the coming days, with consensus forecasts.
- How much the market has moved on similar releases in the past, and how sensitive rates and FX have been to surprises recently.
- Which data points matter most this month, given the regime. In a high-inflation regime, CPI and wage data dominate. In a slowdown, payrolls and jobless claims matter more.
- Scenario bands: what the curve and currencies would do under a hot, in-line, and cool print.

### 4. Curve and rates shape

- Level, slope, and curvature of the main curves: US, euro area, UK, Japan, and the key EM curves.
- Front-end pricing versus long-end pricing, to see whether the market is repricing policy or term premium.
- Term premium estimates and what is driving them: supply, quantitative tightening, and foreign official demand.
- Real rates and breakevens, to separate growth and inflation contributions to nominal moves.

### 5. Inflation and price pressures

- Core and headline inflation trends, and the components driving each.
- Services inflation, shelter, and wage growth, since these are the sticky parts.
- Commodity prices and their pass-through into headline inflation.
- Inflation expectations from surveys and from market breakevens, and whether they are anchored.

### 6. Growth and labour

- Leading indicators: purchasing managers' indices, new orders, credit conditions, and housing activity.
- Labour market tightness: payrolls, unemployment, job openings, quits, and hours worked.
- Consumer demand indicators, such as retail sales and real income growth.
- Any early signs of recession, such as the yield curve, credit spreads, and claims.

### 7. Fiscal and political backdrop

- The fiscal path: deficits, issuance plans, and upcoming auctions.
- Elections, budget decisions, and sanctions that could change supply, demand, or policy.
- For emerging markets, reserve adequacy, external financing needs, and capital flow trends.
- Geopolitical developments that affect energy, trade, or currency flows.

### 8. Currencies and cross-market relationships

- The dollar index and its drivers: rate differentials, safe-haven demand, and positioning.
- Major currency pairs against their fundamentals, and any intervention risk.
- Real effective exchange rate levels for the main currencies.
- Cross-asset relationships that have changed recently, such as the stock-bond correlation, and what that implies for hedges.

### 9. Commodities and energy

- Oil fundamentals: inventories, production, refinery demand, and spare capacity.
- The futures curve shape and what it says about physical tightness.
- Natural gas and power, where seasonality and storage matter.
- Metals and agriculture, depending on the book, with attention to supply shocks.

### 10. Funding, liquidity, and plumbing

- Repo rates versus the policy rate, and any month-end or tax-date pressures.
- Reserve levels and the central bank balance sheet trajectory.
- The cross-currency basis and dollar funding conditions.
- Dealer balance sheet capacity, and whether liquidity in key markets is thinning.

Experienced traders watch this section closely because funding stress often shows up before price moves.

### 11. Volatility and options

- Implied volatility levels across rates, FX, and equities, compared with realised volatility.
- The volatility surface: skew, term structure, and any unusual changes in risk reversals.
- Dealer positioning in options and the strikes where gamma is concentrated.
- The cost of hedges, and whether it is cheap enough to justify protection.

### 12. Positioning, flows, and sentiment

- Speculative positioning from CFTC and other surveys, looking for crowding.
- Fund flows into bonds, equities, and currencies.
- Sentiment indicators, used as context rather than signals on their own.
- Where a consensus trade is vulnerable to a reversal.

### 13. Risk and portfolio review

- The current book: DV01 by curve bucket, FX delta, commodity exposure, and vega.
- Scenario results for the main macro risks, and how the book behaves in each.
- Stop levels, drawdown status against limits, and any positions close to their exit points.
- Correlations between positions, to check that they are not all reliant on the same macro driver.

### 14. Trade ideas and their evidence

- Each idea stated with its thesis, catalyst, entry, stop, target, and what would prove it wrong.
- The expected return against the risk, and the liquidity of the instrument.
- Whether the idea is a view on the level, the curve, relative value, or volatility.
- A review of past ideas: which worked, which failed, and why.

### 15. Things to question every day

Senior traders also keep a short list of assumptions they are checking:

- Is my regime view still right, or am I anchored to a view that the data has already moved past?
- Is the market reacting to news I have not yet understood?
- Are my positions relying on one factor more than I think?
- What would change my mind today?

### How the morning usually looks

1. Overnight moves in Asia and Europe, and what drove them.
2. Regime and policy path check.
3. The day's data and central bank events, with scenario bands.
4. Positioning, flows, and funding conditions.
5. Book review: risk, stops, and exposures.
6. Trade ideas for the day, with the evidence for each.
7. Close-of-day review, and an update to the regime view if the data has changed it.

The purpose of all these views is to answer one question each morning: has anything changed that should change what I am doing? Most days the answer is no, and a good trader can say why.

## First, a correction on swaps

Long swap rates are not driven by government bond yields. Both are priced off the same expected policy path and term premium, but they are separate instruments. A swap rate is the fixed leg that equates the present value of fixed and floating payments, and the floating leg is referenced to overnight rates (SOFR, ESTR, SONIA). The gap between swap rates and government yields is the swap spread, which reflects dealer balance-sheet capacity, collateral scarcity, and funding costs. That spread has been negative for long stretches, meaning swaps sit below Treasuries. So the government curve is a reference point, not the source of the swap rate.

## Why a long-tenor sovereign yield is what it is

The New York Fed's Treasury term premium work splits a long yield into two pieces: expectations of the future path of short-term Treasury yields and the Treasury term premium. The term premium is the compensation that investors require for bearing the risk that short-term Treasury yields do not evolve as they expected. So a 10-year yield equals the expected average of short rates over ten years, plus compensation for uncertainty about that path.

For a new country, that decomposition needs two extra layers, because the bond carries default, currency, and liquidity risk that a mature government bond does not. The underwriter's job is to add up each layer and then add the concession needed to get the deal done.

## The Stage 1 MECE framework

Each component answers a different question, and none of them overlaps.

| Component | Question it answers | Driven by |
|---|---|---|
| A. Expected policy path | Where will short rates average over the tenor? | Current policy rate, neutral rate, how fast policy converges |
| B. Term premium | What extra return compensates for holding duration? | Inflation uncertainty, duration supply, rate volatility, investor-base fragility |
| C. Credit spread | What compensation is there for the chance of default or restructuring? | Debt, debt service, reserves, growth, inflation, external balance, institutions, currency mismatch |
| D. Currency and convertibility premium | What compensates for FX and convertibility risk on a local bond? | Exchange-rate regime, FX volatility, capital controls |
| E. Liquidity premium | What compensates for thin trading and a small investor base? | Market depth, issue size, index eligibility |
| F. New-issue concession | What discount does the underwriter need to sell the deal? | Demand conditions, deal size, competing supply |

Two checks keep the split clean. Expected inflation sits in A, while inflation uncertainty sits in B, so the two don't double count. Credit (C) measures the probability of not being repaid in the bond's currency, while currency (D) measures the cost of converting that currency, so they are separate.

## Weights and where they come from

The weights are my calibrated judgements, informed by the research I found. They are not regression coefficients, so treat them as a starting point rather than an estimate.

- **Credit block (C):** the variable set follows panel studies of emerging-market spreads. One Colombian central bank study found that significant explanatory variables include the economic growth rate, the debt-to-GDP ratio, the reserves-to-GDP ratio, and the debt-to-exports ratio. A follow-up study identifies some seven variables that play a role in determining ratings, creditworthiness and spreads, including inflation, external-debt ratios, debt-service ratios, reserves, and openness.
- **Default spread anchor:** Damodaran builds the sovereign default spread from the rating or CDS. If the government is perceived to have default risk, the government bond rate will have a default spread component in it and not be riskfree. The model uses this logic to map the credit score to a spread.
- **Term premium block (B):** the ACM model is the standard decomposition. Its estimates have changed sign historically; the ten-year term premium was negative at times in the 1960s, but reverted back to positive. For a new country, the wider range is what matters.
- **Concession (F):** new sovereign lines are generally priced against interpolated references with a modest premium. One syndication analysis notes that new lines of this kind are usually priced off interpolated references with a modest new-issue concession.

## The HTML model

The file has four blocks of sliders (A to D) plus the policy-path and tenor inputs. For each factor you set a weight and a risk score from 0 (best) to 100 (worst). The model normalises the weights, converts each block into a score, and maps the score to basis points:

- Credit spread maps exponentially from 40 bp to 1,200 bp.
- Term premium maps linearly to 0–250 bp at 10 years, then scales with tenor.
- Currency premium maps to 0–300 bp.
- Liquidity premium maps to 0–100 bp.

The expected policy path converges from the current rate to the long-run neutral rate. Its average over the tenor depends on the half-life, which is why long tenors move less than short ones when policy changes.

With the default inputs (policy rate 15%, neutral rate 6%, half-life two years, ten-year tenor), the model gives a 10-year coupon of about 14.125%. That is roughly 8.5% from the policy path, about 1.3% term premium, about 2.5% credit spread, about 1.2% currency premium, and about 0.5% liquidity premium, plus a 20 bp concession. Treat this as an illustration of the arithmetic, not a market view.

## Stage 2: spread over the sovereign

For a corporate or quasi-sovereign issuer, the issuer's yield equals the sovereign fair yield at the same tenor, plus an issuer spread, plus the issuer's own concession. The issuer spread is a second scorecard:

| Block | Question it answers | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer financial strength | Can the issuer service its debt from its own cash flow? | 30% |
| Sovereign linkage | How much does the sovereign's credit limit the issuer? | 15% |
| Structural terms | Does the bond's seniority, covenants, or call features change the risk? | 15% |
| Sector macro sensitivity | How exposed is the sector to the cycle and to FX mismatch? | 20% |
| Market technicals | How much supply is there in the sector, and how liquid is the issuer's bond? | 20% |

The sovereign-linkage factor takes its score automatically from the Stage 1 credit block, so a weaker sovereign pushes up every issuer's spread. This follows the usual practice of treating the sovereign as a ceiling for domestic issuers.

## The complete maths

Everything in the model reduces to one additive identity plus a few mappings. I'll write each piece out so you can check it against the HTML.

### Notation

- n: tenor in years
- c: current policy rate (%)
- ℓ: long-run neutral policy rate (%)
- h: half-life of policy convergence (years)
- τ = 6: shape parameter for the tenor build-up of the term premium (a fixed assumption, not estimated)
- For each block X ∈ {credit, term premium, FX, liquidity, issuer}, factors i = 1…k_X with raw weights w_i ≥ 0 and risk scores s_i ∈ [0, 100]

### Step 1: normalise the weights

$$\tilde w_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad \sum_i \tilde w_i = 1$$

If all raw weights are zero, the denominator is set to 1 so the formula doesn't divide by zero.

### Step 2: block risk score

$$S_X = \sum_i \tilde w_i \, s_i \in [0, 100]$$

This is a weighted average of the factor scores, so each block's score stays between 0 and 100.

### Step 3: map each block score to basis points

Credit (exponential, convex):

$$\text{Spread}_{cr}(S) = 40 \cdot 30^{S/100} \quad\text{bp}$$

This gives 40 bp at S = 0 and 1,200 bp at S = 100. The exponent makes the spread rise faster at weaker scores, which matches how spreads behave across rating buckets.

Term premium at 10 years (linear):

$$TP_{10}(S) = 250 \cdot \frac{S}{100} \quad\text{bp}$$

Currency premium (linear):

$$FX(S) = 300 \cdot \frac{S}{100} \quad\text{bp}$$

Liquidity premium (linear):

$$LIQ(S) = 100 \cdot \frac{S}{100} \quad\text{bp}$$

### Step 4: expected policy path over the tenor

The policy rate converges exponentially from c to ℓ with decay rate λ = ln 2 / h. The average over n years is

$$\bar r(n) = \ell + (c - \ell)\cdot\frac{1 - e^{-\lambda n}}{\lambda n}$$

The fraction is 1 when λn → 0, so the code handles that limit explicitly.

### Step 5: tenor shape of the term premium

$$TP(n) = TP_{10}\cdot\frac{1 - e^{-n/\tau}}{1 - e^{-10/\tau}}$$

The denominator makes TP(10) equal TP₁₀ exactly. A long bond carries more duration risk, so the premium rises with tenor but flattens out.

### Step 6: fair yield, reoffer yield, coupon

$$y^{fair}(n) = 100\,\bar r(n) + TP(n) + Spread_{cr}(S_{cr}) + FX(S_{fx}) + LIQ(S_{liq}) \quad\text{bp}$$

$$y^{reoffer}(n) = y^{fair}(n) + \text{NIC}$$

$$\text{Coupon} = \frac{\text{round}\!\left(8\cdot y^{reoffer}/100\right)}{8}\%$$

Rounding to the nearest 1/8% is a market convention for coupon setting. A bond issued at par has coupon equal to its reoffer yield, rounded.

### Step 7: issuer spread (Stage 2)

$$Spread_{iss} = 30 \cdot 30^{S_{iss}/100} \quad\text{bp}$$

$$y^{issuer}_{reoffer} = y^{fair}(n) + Spread_{iss} + \text{NIC}_{iss}$$

The sovereign-linkage factor inside S_iss is set equal to S_cr, so the issuer block depends on the sovereign's credit score.

### Step 8: why the components add

Yields add because each premium is a compensation in return terms for a separate risk, and a holder of the bond earns all of them. The additive form is a first-order approximation. It is exact when the risks are independent and the premia are small relative to the yield. For a stressed sovereign, the risks interact (a default pushes the currency down, and that pushes inflation up), so the true relationship is not additive, and the model understates the tail.

## What I included, what I left out, and why

### Included, and why

- **Expected policy path.** It is the largest single component of any long yield and the one the market prices most directly.
- **Term premium.** The ACM decomposition shows that the term premium is a separate, large, and volatile piece of a long yield, so leaving it out would misprice the tenor.
- **Credit.** A new sovereign's probability of not repaying is the first thing any buyer asks about. The variables follow what panel studies of emerging-market spreads find significant.
- **Currency and convertibility.** For a local-currency bond, the investor is exposed to the FX regime and to the risk of being unable to convert or repatriate funds. Neither shows up in the credit block.
- **Liquidity.** A small, illiquid bond must pay more to find a buyer, and new issuers are usually small.
- **Concession.** The reoffer yield is what investors are offered, and the concession is what the underwriter adds to clear the deal.

### Left out, and why

- **Inflation as a separate block.** Expected inflation is already inside the policy path, and inflation uncertainty is inside the term premium. Adding it again would double count. I did keep inflation as a credit input (inflation level and stability) because a high inflation record affects default risk through the central bank's credibility and the fiscal position.
- **Tax treatment and withholding.** Relevant for foreign investors, but it shifts the required pre-tax yield by a country-specific amount rather than changing the risk. Omitted because it belongs to the investor's after-tax calculation, not the issuer's pricing.
- **Index inclusion flows and benchmark rebalancing.** These matter for flows and short-term technicals, but they are not stable enough to model as a long-run premium. I captured the lasting part (index eligibility) inside the liquidity block.
- **Carry and roll-down of the curve.** These are returns to a holder over time, not something that sets the issue yield.
- **Dealer balance-sheet and swap-spread effects.** Important for the swap market and for the basis, but the sovereign's issue yield is set against cash government curves, not swaps. They enter through the issuer spread indirectly.
- **Geopolitical event risk as a separate factor.** It is real, but in a model like this it belongs in the credit and currency scores (for example, sanctions risk on convertibility) rather than as its own block. A separate block would be hard to score consistently.
- **Commodity prices as a separate factor.** Export concentration already enters through the external balance and debt-to-exports inputs, so a separate commodity input would overlap.

## Is it simplified, or close to reality?

It is simplified, and it is close to reality in structure but not in precision.

### What is close to reality

- The decomposition of a long yield into expected policy path plus term premium is the standard, widely used one.
- The components are the ones real pricing committees discuss: rates path, duration compensation, sovereign risk, FX risk, and liquidity.
- The concession step matches how new issues are actually priced.

### What is simplified

- **Weights are judgements.** Real pricing uses fitted coefficients estimated from observed bonds, and the weights would differ across countries and regimes.
- **Mappings are fixed functional forms.** The exponential and linear maps are chosen for convenience. Real spreads have regime shifts, thresholds (a rating cliff, or a reserve level below which a peg breaks), and jumps that a smooth map misses.
- **Additivity.** Risks interact, especially in stress, so the true relationship is nonlinear.
- **No time dimension.** The model is a snapshot. It doesn't capture how a factor's effect changes over the cycle, or how expectations about it evolve.
- **No uncertainty bands.** Each output is a point estimate. A real pricing desk would work with a distribution.
- **The term premium is set by a rule, not estimated.** A new country has no long history of curve data to fit a model such as ACM.

A fair way to describe it: the model gives the right skeleton and the right direction of every sensitivity, but the numbers are only as good as the weights and mappings you calibrate.

## Would a reinforcement-learning model on a macro database reach high accuracy?

Not automatically, and probably not in the way the question implies. A few points matter.

**Data is the first constraint, not the model.** Macro histories are short for most countries. Many emerging markets have a few dozen usable observations of long-tenor local bond yields, and their regimes have changed (currency regimes, central bank frameworks, default histories). Each country's data is effectively a small sample, and the regime changes break the patterns a model would learn.

**Reinforcement learning is the wrong tool for this problem.** RL learns a policy by trying actions and receiving rewards. Pricing a bond is a prediction problem: given inputs, estimate the yield the market will clear at. The natural tools are regression, state-space or affine term-structure models, and panel methods, with explicit uncertainty. An RL agent would need a simulator of the market to train against, and that simulator would be the same model you are trying to build.

**Learning what markets priced is not the same as learning what is right.** A model trained on past prices learns the market's past pricing, including its mistakes and its regime-specific biases. If the market mispriced a risk historically, the model learns the mistake. Prediction accuracy on past data does not establish that the model captures causation.

**Overfitting is the main risk.** With many factors and few independent episodes, a flexible model will fit the historical data closely and forecast poorly. The effective sample size is the number of distinct regimes, not the number of months.

**What would actually improve accuracy:**

- Pool data across many countries and use panel methods, so each country borrows strength from the others.
- Fit the weights and mappings statistically, with out-of-sample tests across regimes, rather than setting them by hand.
- Use the ACM-type decomposition on the countries that have liquid curves, and calibrate the term premium on those.
- Report prediction intervals, not point estimates, and test calibration: do the 80% intervals contain the outcome 80% of the time?
- Include a regime variable, so the model can shift between a managed-peg regime and a free-float regime.
- Validate on episodes the model did not see, such as a sudden stop or a currency crisis, before trusting it in one.

**The honest answer:** a well-specified statistical model fitted to a large, pooled, carefully checked dataset can get a useful estimate with calibrated uncertainty. It will not produce precise numbers that are reliably correct, because the drivers change over time and the number of truly comparable episodes is small. The most useful output is the distribution of plausible yields and the sensitivity of the number to each factor, which is what the sliders show.
